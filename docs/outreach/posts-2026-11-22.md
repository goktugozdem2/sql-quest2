At SQL Quest, we tie product decisions to a rule written before we see the data. This week one of those rules reached its verdict, and I would like to share it.

For a long time, someone opening the app for the first time was met with a short placement quiz. The intention was sound: start everyone on a question suited to their level. Placement is a long and respected tradition in good teaching, and we still stand behind that intention.

One question remained open, though. Should a newcomer meet a quiz first, or a SQL question straight away? Since late September, new arrivals have been split at random into two groups. Before we began, we wrote the rule down: if the people who met a question solved less, or left without solving anything, the quiz would come back.

The result was clear. Among those who met the quiz, 15.5% solved a question within their first ten minutes. Among those who met a question directly, 37.2% did. Each group held more than five hundred people. Fewer people left without solving anything, too.

As of this week, every newcomer's first screen is a question.

We do not read this as a verdict against placement quizzes. What we read is this: someone preparing for an interview knows what their time is worth. Putting the work in front of them, rather than asking them something first, is a way of respecting that time. Their first few solves already tell us their level.

At SQL Quest, before a change goes live, we also write down the result that would make us take it back. This week one of those rules ruled against an idea of our own.

After someone solved a question, we had begun suggesting their next one from their weakest skill. The idea looked sound; personalisation is the heart of the product. Before we started, we wrote: if the share of people who open the suggested question falls below seventy percent of the old, ordered suggestion, we go back.

It fell. The share who opened the suggestion went from 40.8% to 17.3%. We turned the feature off this week; the suggestion after a solve follows the curriculum order again. The weakest-skill picker stays where the person chooses it themselves, on their own practice card.

This is how we read it: showing someone their hardest topic in the moment right after a win, however well meant, may not fit that moment. Working on a weak spot may belong to the moment a person chooses it. This time, people's own choices proved more accurate than our assumption.

That is the real value of writing these rules in advance. When the data arrives, we do not debate what we would like to believe; we keep the promise we already made.
