Payments interviews ask SQL about money in motion: a card authorisation that may or may not settle, a refund or reversal that undoes it, an amount in one of a dozen currencies, and two systems that should agree about the same transfer. Each of those has a plausible wrong answer that runs without an error. This guide works four of them on a synthetic multi-currency ledger, with every query run and its result shown.

What payments SQL questions look like

A payments company's core table is a list of attempts: who paid whom, how much, in what currency, and what happened. Questions start from it and move to ratios, money in a common currency, and checks between two sources. The public material we have read describes these shapes, paraphrased:

  • Approval and decline shares. A candidate report in Interview Query's Stripe data analyst guide (stamped Interviewed Q4 2026, read 22 November 2026) describes each day's approval share and decline share from a payments table, and merchants ranked by chargeback rate over the last 30 days.
  • Money between users. DataLemur's PayPal set (7 January 2025) includes pairs of users who have sent money in both directions, and each account's closing balance from deposits and withdrawals.
  • Reconciliation. The Stripe data engineer guide on the same site (report stamped Interviewed Q4 2026, read 22 November 2026) describes finding and explaining the records that do not match between two sources.

These are shapes from dated public sources, not questions any company has confirmed, and nothing here says how often they come up. The three questions below are our own, on our own data.

Question 1: authorised, settled, reversed

Question: for card payments, compare domestic and cross-border spend: attempts, how many were authorised, how many settled, and the reversal rate. The words matter. An authorised payment is one the issuer approved; it may still be reversed later (a refund or a cancelled authorisation), so it never settles. A declined payment was never authorised. Three statuses, three different numbers:

one row per corridor, three outcomes, two rates
SELECT CASE WHEN t.merchant_country = u.country THEN 'domestic'
            ELSE 'cross_border' END AS corridor,
       COUNT(*) AS attempts,
       SUM(t.status <> 'declined') AS authorised,
       SUM(t.status = 'completed') AS settled,
       SUM(t.status = 'reversed') AS reversed,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(t.status <> 'declined') / COUNT(*), 1) AS auth_rate_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(t.status = 'reversed') / SUM(t.status <> 'declined'), 1) AS reversal_rate_pct
FROM transactions t
JOIN users u ON u.user_id = t.user_id
WHERE t.type = 'card_payment'
GROUP BY corridor
ORDER BY corridor;

domestic1536149814782097.51.3

Each rate has its own denominator, and that is what the interviewer is checking. The authorisation rate is over attempts. The reversal rate is over authorised payments, because a declined payment cannot be reversed; divide by attempts and the rate looks lower than it is. Filter type = 'card_payment' before any of it: a transfer_in is never declined, so leaving it in would push every approval rate up. And note 100.0: with 100, SQLite divides integers and the rate rounds down to a whole number.

On this ledger the two corridors authorise at almost the same rate, 97.6% and 97.5%. Say that out loud too: a question built to find a difference is also allowed to find none.

Question 2: one total across twelve currencies

Question: what was total settled card spend? Card payments here are charged in euros, pounds, złoty, forints, yen and more. amount is in the currency of the charge, so summing it adds 1,000 forints to 1,000 pounds:

wrong: summing amounts in twelve currencies
SELECT ROUND(SUM(amount), 2) AS card_spend
FROM transactions
WHERE type = 'card_payment' AND status = 'completed';

That returns 878208.96.

right: one currency, settled only
SELECT ROUND(SUM(amount_gbp), 2) AS card_spend_gbp
FROM transactions
WHERE type = 'card_payment' AND status = 'completed';

That returns 106444.06.

The wrong total is about eight times the right one, almost all of it from currencies with small units. If a table has no converted column, join a rates table on the currency code and multiply, and ask which rate is meant: the rate on the day of the payment or one rate for the whole report. Both are defensible; picking one silently is not.

Question 3: reconcile the two legs of a transfer

Question: every completed transfer between two users should appear twice: a transfer_out on the sender and a transfer_in on the receiver. Find the outgoing transfers with no matching incoming leg. This is reconciliation in miniature, and the trap is the matching key. A user paying in pounds can send to a user whose account is in euros, so the two legs carry different amount values for the same money:

match on the charged amount, then on the converted amount
SELECT o.status,
       COUNT(*) AS transfers_out,
       SUM(NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM transactions i
                       WHERE i.type = 'transfer_in'
                         AND i.user_id = o.counterparty_user_id
                         AND i.counterparty_user_id = o.user_id
                         AND i.ts = o.ts
                         AND i.amount = o.amount)) AS unmatched_on_amount,
       SUM(NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM transactions i
                       WHERE i.type = 'transfer_in'
                         AND i.user_id = o.counterparty_user_id
                         AND i.counterparty_user_id = o.user_id
                         AND i.ts = o.ts
                         AND i.amount_gbp = o.amount_gbp)) AS unmatched_on_gbp
FROM transactions o
WHERE o.type = 'transfer_out'
GROUP BY o.status
ORDER BY transfers_out DESC;

completed3452090

Matched on the charged amount, 209 completed transfers look broken, out of 345. Matched on the converted amount, none are, and every unmatched row is a transfer that was declined or reversed, which is exactly what should have no incoming leg. A good reconciliation answer has that shape: the exceptions are few, and each one has a reason you can name. When the match rate looks terrible, suspect the key before the data.

NOT EXISTS keeps one row per outgoing transfer, so the counts cannot fan out the way a LEFT JOIN to a leg with duplicate rows would. To check both directions (incoming legs with no outgoing one), run the mirror query. For the pattern in general, see the SQL anti-join.

FAQ

What SQL questions do fintech and payments companies ask?

Public candidate reports and prep guides describe approval and decline shares from a payments table, chargeback or dispute rates per merchant over a recent window, money sent between users, account balances from deposits and withdrawals, and reconciling records between two sources. Companies do not publish their questions, so treat any list as reported, not official.

What is the difference between authorised and settled in payments SQL?

An authorised payment is one the issuer approved; a settled one actually moved money. A payment can be authorised and later reversed, so it never settles. In SQL that usually means three statuses and three counts: attempts, authorised (everything not declined) and settled (completed), with the reversal rate computed over authorised payments, not over attempts.

How do you sum money across currencies in SQL?

Never sum the charged amount across currencies. Use a converted column if the table has one, or join a rates table on the currency code and multiply, and decide whether the rate is the one on the day of the payment or a single rate for the report.
