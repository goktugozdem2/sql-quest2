What e-commerce SQL questions look like

The core tables are customers, orders, order lines and products, sometimes inventory and returns. Questions measure a business: revenue that counts, customers who return, products that move together, stock that runs out. The public material we have read describes shapes like these, paraphrased:

  • A share against a benchmark. A candidate report on PracHub for an Amazon business intelligence engineer screen (interview September 2026, recorded in our read of 22 November 2026) describes groups where more than half of members beat a target while the group's growth lags the company-wide average, year over year: conditional aggregation for the share, a company-level benchmark in a CTE.
  • Metrics and reporting. Amazon's own interview-prep page for business intelligence engineers names SQL, and metrics and reporting, among the competencies it tests (read 22 November 2026).
  • Inventory windows. Exponent's Walmart data analyst guide (updated mid-2026) describes total sales for each product since its most recent restock.

These are shapes from dated public sources, not questions any retailer has confirmed, and nothing here says how often they come up. The three questions below are our own, on our own data.

The shop used below

A deliberately small shop, so every number can be checked by eye: 16 customers (customer_id, name, signup_date, membership) and 40 orders (order_id, customer_id, product, category, quantity, price, total, order_date, country, status) from January and February 2024, in two categories, Electronics and Furniture. Each order holds one product, and there is no returns table; both limits change how the questions below are asked, and the page says where. Every query runs on SQLite, as in the browser.

Question 1: GMV, net revenue and order value

Question: what did the shop sell, and what was the average order value? "Sold" has at least two meanings. Gross merchandise value (GMV) counts every order placed; net revenue counts the orders that completed. Pending and cancelled orders are in the table either way. One query can show both, with the status in a CASE instead of a WHERE, so the reader sees what the filter removed:

gross and net side by side
SELECT COUNT(*) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(total), 2) AS gross_gmv,
       SUM(status = 'completed') AS completed_orders,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN status = 'completed' THEN total END), 2) AS net_revenue,
       ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN status = 'completed' THEN total END), 2) AS aov
FROM orders;
ordersgross_gmvcompleted_ordersnet_revenueaov
408156.44377971.51215.45

The average order value uses AVG(CASE WHEN … THEN total END) with no ELSE, so non-completed orders become NULL and drop out of both the sum and the count. Write ELSE 0 and they would stay in the count as zero-value orders, pulling the average down. On real order-line data, AOV also needs the order grain: sum the lines per order first, then average the orders, or a three-line order counts three times.

Question 2: a repeat purchase rate, and its denominator

Question: what share of customers bought again? The numerator is clear: customers with two or more completed orders. The denominator is a choice. Customers who bought at least once is the usual meaning of repeat rate; all registered customers answers a different question, closer to activation. Build one row per customer, keeping those with no orders through a LEFT JOIN, then compute both:

one row per customer, two denominators
WITH per_customer AS (
  SELECT c.customer_id, COUNT(o.order_id) AS orders
  FROM customers c
  LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id AND o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY c.customer_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers,
       SUM(orders >= 1) AS buyers,
       SUM(orders >= 2) AS repeat_buyers,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(orders >= 2) / SUM(orders >= 1), 1) AS repeat_rate_of_buyers,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(orders >= 2) / COUNT(*), 1) AS repeat_rate_of_customers
FROM per_customer;
customersbuyersrepeat_buyersrepeat_rate_of_buyersrepeat_rate_of_customers
16101010062.5

100% and 62.5% are both correct, for different questions; say which one you computed. Three details carry the answer. COUNT(o.order_id) counts matched orders only, so a customer with none gets 0, where COUNT(*) would give them 1. The status filter sits in the ON clause, so customers whose only order was cancelled stay in the table with zero orders; in a WHERE it would delete them (see the LEFT JOIN WHERE filter trap). And a real repeat-rate question usually adds a window, such as a second order within 30 days of the first, which needs each customer's first order date.

Question 3: affinity, with lift and not just confidence

Question: do furniture buyers also buy electronics? Basket analysis normally pairs products inside one order. Here every order holds one product, so the honest version measures affinity across each customer's orders: build one row per buyer with a flag per category, then compare.

flags per buyer, then confidence and lift
WITH flags AS (
  SELECT customer_id,
         MAX(category = 'Furniture') AS furniture,
         MAX(category = 'Electronics') AS electronics
  FROM orders
  WHERE status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS buyers,
       SUM(furniture) AS furniture_buyers,
       SUM(furniture AND electronics) AS both_categories,
       ROUND(1.0 * SUM(furniture AND electronics) / SUM(furniture), 2) AS confidence,
       ROUND(1.0 * SUM(electronics) / COUNT(*), 2) AS electronics_base_rate,
       ROUND((1.0 * SUM(furniture AND electronics) / SUM(furniture))
             / (1.0 * SUM(electronics) / COUNT(*)), 2) AS lift
FROM flags;
buyersfurniture_buyersboth_categoriesconfidenceelectronics_base_ratelift
10850.630.70.89

Confidence says 63% of furniture buyers also bought electronics, which sounds like a strong pairing. Lift says otherwise: 70% of all buyers bought electronics, so furniture buyers are slightly less likely than average, a lift of 0.89. A lift above 1 is an affinity; below 1 is the opposite. Interviewers ask about lift precisely because confidence alone rewards whatever is popular. On order-line data the same idea starts from a self-join of the lines on the order id with a.product < b.product, so each pair appears once and no product pairs with itself.

MAX(category = 'Furniture') turns many orders into one yes-or-no per customer. Summing the comparison instead would count a customer with three furniture orders three times, and the ratios would stop being shares of people.

Returns by cohort: the shape to know

This shop has no returns table, so here is the shape in words rather than a result. A returns question joins returns to the orders they reverse, then groups by a cohort, usually the customer's first-order month or signup month. Two traps: divide returned orders by orders placed in the same cohort, not by all orders in the period the return happened; and count a partially returned order once, by order id, unless the question asks for units. It is the same cohort skeleton as a retention table, which you can practise in Customer Retention Cohort.

FAQ

What SQL questions do e-commerce companies ask data analysts?

Public candidate reports and prep guides describe revenue and order-value questions, shares of customers or groups against a benchmark, repeat purchase and retention cohorts, top products per category, and inventory questions such as sales since the last restock. Companies do not publish their questions, so treat any list as reported, not official.

What is the difference between GMV and net revenue in SQL?

GMV sums every order placed; net revenue sums only the orders that count, usually completed ones, after cancellations and returns. In SQL, put the status in a CASE inside the aggregate to show both side by side, and leave out ELSE 0 when averaging so excluded orders drop out of the count.

How do you calculate a repeat purchase rate in SQL?

Build one row per customer with a LEFT JOIN to their completed orders and COUNT(order_id), then divide customers with two or more orders by customers with at least one. Dividing by all registered customers answers a different question; say which denominator you used.
