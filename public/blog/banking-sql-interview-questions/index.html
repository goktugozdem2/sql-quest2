Bank and card-issuer analyst interviews ask SQL about money that is owed, late, or disputed. The shapes repeat across employers: put accounts into risk buckets, track how they move between buckets from one period to the next, and measure how long something takes, such as the time from a charge to a dispute. This guide works each one on real data: the FDIC's public bank filings for 2025 and a synthetic card ledger. Every query below was run, and the result under it is what it returns.

What bank analyst SQL questions look like

A retail bank or a card issuer runs on a few tables: accounts or loans, a monthly or quarterly snapshot of each one (balance, days past due, status), transactions, and disputes. Analyst questions are about the movement between those snapshots far more than about any single row. Three shapes come up in the public material we have read:

  • Buckets. Put each account, loan or bank into a band: current, 30 days past due, 60, 90+; or a ratio band. Prep guides for Capital One's data-analyst screen list "CASE WHEN for conditional metrics and buckets" among the topics (extrabrain guide, February 2026; see Sources).
  • Movement between periods. Which accounts got worse since last month, which recovered, which disappeared. A credit team calls the share that moves to a worse bucket a roll rate. DataLemur's JPMorgan Chase set (updated 28 March 2025) includes accounts that were very active in a recent window or dormant over a longer one, and a card product's best and worst month of issuance: both are period-over-period questions.
  • Reconciliation and timing. Interview Query's JPMorgan Chase data analyst guide (21 candidate reports, updated 20 November 2026, read 24 November 2026) says one candidate who accepted an offer was asked about reconciling data across reports and software. Card teams add timing: how many days from a charge to its dispute, and how long a dispute stays open.

Those are paraphrased shapes from dated public sources, not questions any bank has confirmed, and none of them says how often a topic comes up. What follows is how to answer each shape correctly, and the mistake that makes a plausible wrong answer.

The data used below

Account-level delinquency data is private, so no public dataset has days-past-due per loan. The mechanics are the same one level up. The first two questions use financials from the FDIC's BankFind data: one row per bank per quarter-end for the four quarters of 2025, with each bank's non-performing loan ratio (npl_ratio, in percent). A bank's NPL ratio is the share of its loans that have stopped performing, so bucketing banks by it is the same SQL as bucketing loans by days past due. The third question uses a synthetic card ledger: transactions (txn_id, account_id, amount, txn_at, merchant_id, …) and chargebacks (chargeback_id, txn_id, reason_code, opened_at, resolved_at, status, …). Both run on SQLite, as they do in the browser.

Question 1: risk buckets, and the CASE that swallows them

Question: how many banks fall into each NPL band at the end of 2025: under 0.5%, 0.5–1%, 1–2%, and 2% or more? The natural first draft walks the thresholds upward with >=:

wrong: the first WHEN catches everything above 0.5
SELECT CASE WHEN npl_ratio >= 0.5 THEN '0.5-1%'
            WHEN npl_ratio >= 1 THEN '1-2%'
            WHEN npl_ratio >= 2 THEN '2%+'
            ELSE 'under 0.5%' END AS npl_bucket,
       COUNT(*) AS banks
FROM financials
WHERE period_end = '20251231'
GROUP BY npl_bucket
ORDER BY npl_bucket;
npl_bucketbanks
0.5-1%87
under 0.5%106

Two of the four bands are missing. CASE returns the first WHEN that is true, and every bank at 1% or 3% is also at least 0.5%, so the first branch takes them all. Nothing errors; the query just answers a different question. Write the bands so each branch can only see what the earlier ones left, either with upper bounds in rising order or lower bounds in falling order. Then put a sort key in the label, because ORDER BY on text puts "2%+" before "under 0.5%".

right: rising upper bounds, a sortable label
SELECT CASE WHEN npl_ratio < 0.5 THEN '1: under 0.5%'
            WHEN npl_ratio < 1 THEN '2: 0.5-1%'
            WHEN npl_ratio < 2 THEN '3: 1-2%'
            ELSE '4: 2%+' END AS npl_bucket,
       COUNT(*) AS banks
FROM financials
WHERE period_end = '20251231'
GROUP BY npl_bucket
ORDER BY npl_bucket;
npl_bucketbanks
1: under 0.5%106
2: 0.5-1%62
3: 1-2%20
4: 2%+5

The bands add up to the same 193 banks as before; only the split is now true. In an interview, say the boundary rule out loud: is a bank at exactly 1.0% in the 0.5–1% band or the 1–2% band? Here it is in 1–2%, because the test is < 1. A days-past-due bucket has the same question at 30, 60 and 90 days.

Question 2: roll rates between two quarters

Question: of the banks in each NPL band at the end of Q3 2025, how many moved to a better band by the end of Q4, how many stayed, and how many rolled to a worse one? This is a roll-rate table. It needs each bank's band in two periods side by side: bucket once in a CTE, then join the table to itself on the bank, Q3 on the left and Q4 on the right.

bucket once, then join Q3 to Q4 on the bank
WITH b AS (
  SELECT cert, period_end,
         CASE WHEN npl_ratio < 0.5 THEN 1
              WHEN npl_ratio < 1 THEN 2
              WHEN npl_ratio < 2 THEN 3
              ELSE 4 END AS bucket
  FROM financials
)
SELECT q3.bucket AS from_bucket,
       COUNT(*) AS banks,
       SUM(q4.bucket < q3.bucket) AS improved,
       SUM(q4.bucket = q3.bucket) AS stayed,
       SUM(q4.bucket > q3.bucket) AS rolled_worse,
       SUM(q4.cert IS NULL) AS no_q4_row
FROM b q3
LEFT JOIN b q4 ON q4.cert = q3.cert AND q4.period_end = '20251231'
WHERE q3.period_end = '20250930'
GROUP BY q3.bucket
ORDER BY q3.bucket;

Three things in that query are the answer to the follow-up questions an interviewer asks next.

  • Why a LEFT JOIN. One bank in the worst band has no Q4 row. Banks fail, merge or stop filing, as loans are paid off or charged off. An inner join drops it silently, and the band's denominator shrinks from 8 to 7. The no_q4_row column keeps it visible, which is where a reviewer looks first.
  • Why the bucket is a number. Comparing q4.bucket > q3.bucket only means "worse" because the bands are ordered integers. With text labels you would need a second CASE.
  • Why the join condition holds the period. The Q4 filter sits in the ON clause, not in WHERE. In WHERE, q4.period_end = '20251231' would throw away the NULL row the LEFT JOIN just kept: the trap described on the LEFT JOIN WHERE filter page.

A roll rate is then a ratio of two cells: of the 66 banks in band 2 at Q3, 3 rolled worse, 4.5%. On loan data the same table runs month to month, and the classic question is the share of 30-day-late accounts that roll to 60 days.

Question 3: the dispute window, in days

Question: for each chargeback reason, how many days pass on average between the card transaction and the dispute, and how many disputes were opened within 7 days? Card networks give cardholders a limited window to dispute, so issuers watch this closely. Timestamps here are ISO text such as 2026-03-06T16:52:17.429Z, and the tempting first draft subtracts them directly:

wrong: subtracting two text timestamps
SELECT ROUND(AVG(c.opened_at - t.txn_at), 1) AS avg_days_to_dispute
FROM chargebacks c
JOIN transactions t ON t.txn_id = c.txn_id;

That returns 0.

Zero days, for every dispute. SQLite turns each string into the number at its front, 2026, and 2026 minus 2026 is 0. No error warns you. Convert both sides to day numbers with julianday() first (Postgres would subtract timestamps natively; on a CodeSignal-style platform, check which engine you are on):

right: julianday() on both sides
SELECT c.reason_code,
       COUNT(*) AS disputes,
       SUM(julianday(c.opened_at) - julianday(t.txn_at) <= 7) AS within_7_days,
       ROUND(AVG(julianday(c.opened_at) - julianday(t.txn_at)), 1) AS avg_days_to_dispute
FROM chargebacks c
JOIN transactions t ON t.txn_id = c.txn_id
GROUP BY c.reason_code
ORDER BY avg_days_to_dispute, c.reason_code;

Two habits make this answer defensible. The second ORDER BY key matters because two reasons tie at 6.8 days, and without it the order of a tie is up to the engine. And the join goes from the dispute to its transaction, one to one, so nothing fans out; joining disputes to all of an account's transactions would multiply every dispute. For the status of a dispute, read the columns rather than the label: practise that in Chargeback Reason Codes: Resolved and Still Open.

FAQ

What SQL questions do banks ask data analysts?

Public prep material and candidate reports describe three recurring shapes: putting accounts, loans or banks into risk buckets with CASE WHEN; comparing those buckets between two periods, such as which accounts moved to a worse band (a roll rate); and timing or reconciliation questions, such as days from a charge to its dispute or matching totals across two reports. Banks do not publish their questions, so treat any list as reported, not official.

How do you calculate a roll rate in SQL?

Bucket each account once per period in a CTE, then join the CTE to itself on the account: the earlier period on the left, the later period on the right, with the later period named in the ON clause of a LEFT JOIN. Count, per starting bucket, the accounts whose later bucket is higher. Keep accounts with no later row visible in their own column instead of letting an inner join drop them.

Why does my CASE WHEN put everything in one bucket?

CASE returns the first branch that is true. If the first branch is a broad lower bound such as npl_ratio >= 0.5, it also catches every larger value, so the later branches never run. Order the branches so each can only see what the earlier ones left: rising upper bounds with <, or falling lower bounds with >=.
